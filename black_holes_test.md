# Black Holes

## Introduction
A **black hole** is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing—not even light—can escape from it. Black holes form when massive stars collapse under their own gravity at the end of their life cycle, and they can also be created through other processes such as the merger of neutron stars.

## Formation
- **Stellar‑mass black holes**: Created when stars with a mass greater than about 20–25 solar masses exhaust their nuclear fuel and undergo a super‑nova explosion. The core collapses to a singularity.
- **Super‑massive black holes**: Found at the centers of most galaxies, including the Milky Way. Their masses range from millions to billions of solar masses. Their origin is still a topic of research, involving mechanisms like direct collapse of massive gas clouds or the merging of smaller black holes.
- **Intermediate‑mass black holes**: Hypothetical class with masses between stellar and super‑massive, possibly formed by the merger of multiple stellar‑mass black holes or via dense star clusters.

## Structure
1. **Event horizon** – The boundary beyond which nothing can escape. The radius of the event horizon (the Schwarzschild radius) is given by \(R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}\).
2. **Singularity** – A point (or region) of infinite density where known physics breaks down.
3. **Accretion disk** – A rotating disk of gas and dust that spirals into the black hole, heating up and emitting electromagnetic radiation.
4. **Relativistic jets** – High‑speed streams of particles ejected along the black hole’s rotational axis, often observed in active galactic nuclei.

## Types of Black Holes
- **Schwarzschild black hole** – Non‑rotating, spherically symmetric, described by the Schwarzschild metric.
- **Kerr black hole** – Rotating black hole described by the Kerr metric; possesses an ergosphere where space‑time is dragged.
- **Reissner‑Nordström black hole** – Charged black hole; theoretical as astrophysical black holes are expected to be neutral.

## Observational Evidence
- **Gravitational waves**: Detected by LIGO and Virgo from black‑hole mergers (e.g., GW150914).
- **Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)**: Produced the first image of a black‑hole shadow (M87* in 2019).
- **X‑ray binaries**: Systems where a black hole accretes matter from a companion star, producing X‑ray emissions.

## Significance in Physics
- **General relativity**: Black holes are solutions to Einstein’s field equations and provide tests for the theory in extreme conditions.
- **Quantum gravity**: The information‑paradox and Hawking radiation (black‑hole evaporation) are pivotal in attempts to unify quantum mechanics with gravity.
- **Cosmology**: Super‑massive black holes influence galaxy formation and evolution.

## Hawking Radiation
Proposed by Stephen Hawking (1974), black holes can emit particles due to quantum effects near the event horizon, causing them to lose mass over time. For stellar‑mass black holes, this radiation is extremely weak; only tiny black holes would evaporate within the age of the universe.
